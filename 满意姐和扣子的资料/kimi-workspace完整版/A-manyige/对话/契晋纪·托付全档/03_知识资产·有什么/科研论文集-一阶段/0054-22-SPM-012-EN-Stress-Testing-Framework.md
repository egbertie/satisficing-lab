# Paper 12 (EN): A Stress Testing Framework for Partnership Agreements Based on AI Red-Teaming Methodology

## Abstract

Partnership agreements, like software systems, often fail not from expected conditions but from edge cases and adversarial scenarios. This paper adapts AI "red-teaming" methodology — systematic adversarial testing — to partnership agreement design. Drawing on practices from the AI industry (particularly DJI's AI Blue Army approach), we propose a five-dimensional stress testing framework: interest conflict, power struggle, exit scenarios, external shocks, and long-term alignment. The framework provides entrepreneurs with a systematic tool to identify and mitigate partnership vulnerabilities before they become crises.

**Keywords**: AI red-teaming; Stress testing; Partnership agreements; Adversarial testing; Risk management

---

## 1. Introduction

AI systems are tested not by asking "Does it work?" but "How can we make it fail?" This adversarial mindset — "red-teaming" — is standard in AI safety (Ganguli et al., 2022). The same mindset is rarely applied to partnership agreements, yet partnership failure consequences can exceed software failure damages.

This paper adapts AI red-teaming to partnership agreement design. Core insight: agreements should be stress-tested against adversarial scenarios before signing.

---

## 2. AI Red-Teaming: Core Principles

Red-teaming is "rigorously challenging plans, policies, systems, and assumptions by adopting an adversarial approach" (UK Cabinet Office, 2013). In AI, it involves:
- **Adversarial input generation**: Creating inputs to confuse or break the system;
- **Failure mode enumeration**: Systematically identifying all failure modes;
- **Stress testing**: Pushing systems to limits;
- **Boundary exploration**: Defining safe operating envelopes.

Partnerships and AI systems share structural similarities: complex systems with multiple interacting components, operating under uncertainty, with potential catastrophic edge-case failures.

## 3. Five-Dimensional Stress Testing Framework

### 3.1 Dimension 1: Interest Conflict

**Stress Test**: "When valuation drops from $300M to $30M, what does each partner choose?"

**Adversarial Scenarios**:
- Revenue miss by 50%;
- Key patent invalidated;
- Major customer cancellation;
- Funding round fails at last minute.

**Agreement Stress Points**:
- Equity split: Does it reflect actual contribution under stress?
- Vesting schedules: Do they incentivize staying or leaving?
- Anti-dilution: Do protections become weapons under stress?

### 3.2 Dimension 2: Power Struggle

**Stress Test**: "When founder and partner disagree on critical strategic decision, who decides?"

**Adversarial Scenarios**:
- Founder wants pivot; partner wants stay the course;
- Partner wants raise capital; founder wants preserve equity;
- Disagreement on CEO replacement;
- Disagreement on acquisition offer.

**Agreement Stress Points**:
- Decision-making authority: Is it clear who decides what?
- Tie-breaking mechanisms: What happens when votes split?
- Veto rights: Do they prevent deadlock or create it?

### 3.3 Dimension 3: Exit Scenarios

**Stress Test**: "When partner wants to leave, is exit mechanism clear, fair, and executable?"

**Adversarial Scenarios**:
- Partner quits during critical funding round;
- Partner terminated for cause;
- Partner dies or becomes incapacitated;
- Partner wants to start competing business.

**Agreement Stress Points**:
- Buy-sell provisions: Is valuation formula fair under stress?
- Non-compete: Is it enforceable and reasonable?
- IP ownership: Who owns work in progress?
- Client relationships: Can departing partner poach clients?

### 3.4 Dimension 4: External Shocks

**Stress Test**: "When external shock hits (pandemic, regulatory change, competitor breakthrough), does partnership hold or fracture?"

**Adversarial Scenarios**:
- New regulation bans core business model;
- Competitor launches superior product at 50% price;
- Key supplier goes bankrupt;
- Global pandemic disrupts supply chain.

**Agreement Stress Points**:
- Force majeure: Is it defined broadly enough?
- Emergency decision-making: Can partners act quickly without full consensus?
- Cost-cutting authority: Who decides layoffs and budget cuts?

### 3.5 Dimension 5: Long-Term Alignment

**Stress Test**: "In 5 years, will partners still want the same things?"

**Adversarial Scenarios**:
- One wants IPO; other wants acquisition;
- One wants international expansion; other wants domestic focus;
- One wants new investors; other wants maintain control;
- Life changes: marriage, children, health issues.

**Agreement Stress Points**:
- Long-term vision alignment: Is there regular realignment mechanism?
- Succession planning: What if partner can no longer perform?
- Lifestyle expectations: Do partners have compatible work-life goals?

## 4. Stress Testing Protocol

```
1. Agreement Review → 2. Scenario Generation → 3. Stress Application → 4. Failure Analysis → 5. Remediation
```

**Step 1**: Legal team reviews all provisions, identifying ambiguities and gaps.
**Step 2**: Generate 20-30 adversarial scenarios across five dimensions.
**Step 3**: Simulate agreement performance under each scenario.
**Step 4**: Classify failures by severity (critical/major/minor).
**Step 5**: Rewrite provisions, add contingency clauses, retest.

---

## 5. Conclusion

Partnership agreements should be treated like critical software systems — tested for edge cases and adversarial scenarios, not ideal conditions. The five-dimensional stress testing framework provides a systematic approach to identifying vulnerabilities before crises occur.

The red-teaming mindset — "How can we make this fail?" — is uncomfortable but essential. Better to discover fatal flaws during stress testing than during real crises.

As the AI safety community learned: "The question is not whether the system will fail, but when and how." The same applies to partnerships.
