## Abstract

Herbert A. Simon's satisficing theory provides a revolutionary framework for understanding human decision-making under information constraints. This paper systematically applies satisficing theory to the specific context of entrepreneurial partner matching, constructing the "Satisficing Partner Matching Model" (SPMM). Through theoretical derivation and case analysis, we demonstrate that pursuing the "optimal partner" in entrepreneurship is not only impractical but may lead to decision delays and opportunity cost losses. Instead, setting "satisfaction thresholds" and stopping search when thresholds are met represents a more procedurally rational strategy. We also propose a five-dimension evaluation framework providing entrepreneurs with actionable partner assessment tools.

**Keywords**: Satisficing; Bounded rationality; Partner matching; Entrepreneurial decision-making; Herbert Simon

## 1. Introduction

Startup failures stem from multiple causes, but research indicates that partner conflicts are among the most significant (Ucbasaran et al., 2003). In hard-tech commercialization — characterized by high technical barriers, substantial capital requirements, and long development cycles — partnership quality directly determines project success or failure.

Traditional economics assumes decision-makers are fully rational, capable of evaluating all alternatives and selecting utility-maximizing options (von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944). This assumption diverges dramatically from reality. Nobel laureate Herbert A. Simon (1916-2001) proposed the "satisficing" concept in 1955, offering a more realistic framework for understanding real-world decision behavior (Simon, 1955). This paper systematically applies satisficing theory to entrepreneurial partner matching, providing entrepreneurs with a bounded-rationality-based decision toolkit.

## 2. Theoretical Foundation

### 2.1 Bounded Rationality and Satisficing

Simon's bounded rationality concept identifies three constraints on human decision-making: (1) incomplete information; (2) cognitive limitations; (3) time pressure (Simon, 1978). Under these constraints, decision-makers cannot "maximize" utility as classical models assume, but rather "satisfice" — searching for the first option meeting an "aspiration level" and stopping.

Satisficing operates through three steps (Byron, 2005):
1. **Set aspiration level**: The minimum acceptable standard based on goals and constraints;
2. **Sequential search**: Evaluate alternatives in sequence;
3. **Stopping rule**: Stop when the first satisfactory alternative is found.

As Simon noted: "The rational agent chooses the first alternative whose outcomes are all satisfactory... It is a stopping rule" (Simon, 1979, p. 173).

### 2.2 Applicability to Entrepreneurial Decisions

Entrepreneurial environments naturally satisfy satisficing theory's assumptions:
- **High uncertainty**: Future returns are unpredictable;
- **Resource constraints**: Limited time and capital prevent infinite partner search;
- **Cognitive load**: Multi-dimensional partner evaluation exceeds human processing capacity.

Thus, entrepreneurs adopting satisficing strategies are making rational choices within real constraints, not "compromising" (Stevens, 2019).

## 3. The Satisficing Partner Matching Model (SPMM)

### 3.1 Model Structure

SPMM comprises three layers:

**Input Layer**
- Entrepreneur profile: goals, skill gaps, value preferences, risk tolerance;
- Candidate pool: available potential partners;
- Satisfaction thresholds: minimum acceptable standards.

**Processing Layer**
- Five-dimension evaluation: decomposed partner assessment (see 3.2);
- Sequential search: systematic candidate evaluation;
- Threshold judgment: does the candidate meet all dimension thresholds?

**Output Layer**
- Match result: satisfactory/unsatisfactory;
- Decision log: process recording for learning;
- Case accumulation: experience transformation into reusable knowledge.

### 3.2 Five-Dimension Evaluation Framework

| Dimension | Assessment Content | Example Threshold |
|-----------|---------------------|-------------------|
| **Skill Complementarity** | Does candidate possess key skills the entrepreneur lacks? | At least 2 core skills |
| **Value Alignment** | Do core values align? | Alignment on "integrity" and "long-termism" |
| **Risk Tolerance** | Tolerance for uncertainty | Deviation from entrepreneur's tolerance ≤ ±20% |
| **Character Foundation** | Moral quality and professional ethics | No major integrity issues, positive peer reviews |
| **Collaboration History** | Successful team experience | At least 1 complete project cycle |

### 3.3 Stopping Rules

SPMM employs three stopping rules:
- **Single-candidate stop**: When one candidate meets all five dimension thresholds;
- **Global stop**: After evaluating 80% of the pool without finding a satisfactory candidate, reassess thresholds;
- **Time stop**: Hard deadline (e.g., 3 months), selecting the closest-to-threshold candidate.

## 4. Case Analysis

### 4.1 Case A: Excessive Pickiness Failure (Type 07)

Entrepreneur Zhang set extreme standards for a technical co-founder: PhD required, 10+ years experience, successful exit history, low equity acceptance. Over 18 months, Zhang interviewed 50+ candidates, rejecting all. The project died when the funding window closed.

**Analysis**: Zhang fell into the "maximization trap." If SPMM had been applied with reasonable thresholds ("Master's + 5 years + positive references"), Zhang might have stopped at candidate 5, with sufficient time to advance the project.

### 4.2 Case B: Satisficing Success

Entrepreneur Li sought an operations partner using SPMM. Threshold: "5+ years chip industry experience + at least one 0-to-1 team building experience + value alignment (verified through 3 deep conversations)." After evaluating 12 candidates, candidate 7 (Wang) met all thresholds. Li stopped searching, proceeded to a 3-month trial period. Wang became co-founder; the company completed Series B within 2 years.

**Analysis**: Li's success stemmed from accepting "good enough." She did not pursue the "perfect operations partner" but set reasonable thresholds and stopped decisively when met.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Theoretical Contributions

1. Extending satisficing theory from general to specific entrepreneurial contexts;
2. Constructing SPMM as a theory-to-practice bridge;
3. Proposing the five-dimension evaluation framework, transforming abstract "satisfaction" into actionable criteria.

### 5.2 Practical Implications

SPMM provides entrepreneurs with a "stop-loss" mechanism, helping them recognize:
- Searching for "better" candidates has costs;
- Thresholds should reflect personal needs, not external standards;
- Process quality matters more than outcome quality.

### 5.3 Limitations

1. SPMM effectiveness depends on threshold-setting quality;
2. The five-dimension framework may not suit all contexts;
3. Long-term partnership stability outcomes require longitudinal validation.

---

## 6. Conclusion

Satisficing theory offers a realistic decision framework for entrepreneurial partner matching. In information-incomplete, time-limited, cognitively constrained environments, pursuing the "optimal partner" is impractical and may cause opportunity cost losses. Setting reasonable satisfaction thresholds and stopping decisively when met represents a more procedurally rational strategy.

As Simon observed: "Bounded rationality is not a reflection of irrationality — it is still predicated on expected utility, but that utility results from a restricted search for knowledge" (Stevens, 2019). Choosing a partner means choosing someone "good enough" — this itself is wisdom.
